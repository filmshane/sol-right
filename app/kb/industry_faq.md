## How solar works
Q: How do rooftop solar panels work?
A: Panels convert sunlight into DC electricity. An inverter converts DC to AC for your home. You use solar power first; extra production typically exports to the grid under your utility’s net-metering or successor rules.

Q: What is net metering?
A: Net metering (or similar net billing) is a utility arrangement that credits excess solar energy sent to the grid so it can offset power you pull later. Rules and credit value vary by utility and state. Tennessee utility programs differ — a specialist confirms your utility’s current interconnection rules.

Q: Will solar work if it is cloudy or at night?
A: Production drops in heavy cloud cover and stops at night. Your home draws from the grid (or a battery if installed) when solar is not producing. Annual estimates already average weather patterns.

Q: Do I need a battery?
A: Not required for grid-tied solar. Batteries add backup during outages and can increase self-consumption. Whether a battery makes sense depends on outage concerns, utility rates, and budget. Tell Dave if you want backup options.

## Cost, savings, financing
Q: How much does a solar system cost?
A: It depends on system size, roof complexity, equipment, and incentives. Online estimates give production-oriented sizing from your address and bill; a specialist provides pricing after design review. National averages vary widely by market.

Q: What financing options exist?
A: Common options industry-wide are cash purchase, solar loans, and sometimes leases or PPAs (power purchase agreements). Ownership (cash/loan) usually qualifies the homeowner for applicable tax credits; leases/PPAs often mean the company owns the system. SOL-RIGHT discusses cash, loan, and partner options for your situation.

Q: Are “free solar panels” really free?
A: Truly free systems are rare. “$0 down” usually means a loan, lease, or PPA with ongoing payments. Always compare lifetime cost, who owns the system, warranties, and what happens if you sell the home.

Q: What about the federal solar tax credit?
A: The U.S. residential clean energy credit may apply to qualifying owned systems. Percentage and rules change over time and depend on tax liability. We do not give tax advice — confirm with a tax professional. Specialists can discuss current incentive themes at proposal time.

Q: How long until solar pays for itself?
A: Payback depends on system cost, electricity rates, incentives, production, and financing. Many U.S. homeowners see multi-year paybacks; your estimate starts with production vs usage, then a proposal refines economics.

## Roof, home suitability
Q: Is my roof good for solar?
A: South and southwest faces with good sun and limited shade are usually best. Age, material, structure, and shading matter. Google Solar helps with remote potential; a site visit confirms feasibility. Roof replacement is often recommended before solar if the roof is near end of life.

Q: What if I have shading from trees?
A: Shade reduces production. Layouts prioritize sunnier roof segments. Tree trimming, microinverters/optimizers, or smaller arrays may be discussed. Share known shading with Dave.

Q: Can solar damage my roof?
A: Proper racking and flashing protect roof integrity when installed correctly. SOL-RIGHT emphasizes clean flashings and code-compliant work. Workmanship warranties cover install quality; manufacturer warranties cover equipment.

Q: What if I need a new roof soon?
A: Install solar after roof work when possible, or coordinate both. Putting new panels on a roof that needs replacement soon can mean removing and reinstalling panels later.

## Equipment & warranties
Q: How long do solar panels last?
A: Panels are commonly warrantied around 25 years for performance (often still producing a high fraction of original output). Systems can operate longer. Inverters may have shorter warranties (often 10–25 years depending on type).

Q: What warranties should I expect?
A: Industry norms: panel performance ~25 years, inverter 10–25 years, workmanship often 10 years (SOL-RIGHT demo default workmanship 10 years; exact docs with proposal). Confirm final warranty packet before signing.

Q: Who monitors the system?
A: Modern systems include monitoring apps so you and the installer can track production. Report underperformance so it can be diagnosed.

## After solar / selling a home
Q: What happens to my utility bill after solar?
A: You typically still have a utility account. Bills often drop as solar offsets usage; you may still see fixed charges. Export credits depend on utility policy. Battery + rate plans can change the picture.

Q: What if I sell my house?
A: Owned systems can transfer with the home and may be attractive to buyers when paperwork is clean. Leases/PPAs usually require the buyer to assume the agreement. Disclose solar early in a sale.

Q: Can I expand the system later?
A: Sometimes, if roof space, panel capacity, and electrical limits allow. Plan ahead if you expect an EV, heat pump, or addition.

## Safety & quality
Q: Can I install solar myself?
A: DIY is generally not recommended for grid-tied systems due to roof safety, electrical code, permits, and utility interconnection. Licensed installers reduce risk and keep warranties valid.

Q: How do I avoid solar scams?
A: Get clear written proposals, compare multiple offers when possible, verify licensing/insurance, beware high-pressure same-day signing, and understand financing total cost. Use independent calculators as a sanity check, then verify with a local site assessment.
